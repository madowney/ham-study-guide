Every ham ends up measuring things and soldering things. This lesson covers how to use a multimeter without breaking it, and how to make a good solder joint.

Meters

MeasuresInstrumentHow it's connected
Voltage (electric potential)VoltmeterIn parallel, across the component
CurrentAmmeterIn series, so the current flows through the meter
ResistanceOhmmeterAcross the part, with the power off

Recall the series/parallel lesson: parallel parts share the same voltage, so a voltmeter goes in parallel. Series parts share the same current, so an ammeter goes in series.

A multimeter does all of these. The exam's pick is voltage and resistance.

  • An ohmmeter works by applying a small current and measuring the resulting voltage. That's Ohm's law again.
  • So make sure the circuit isn't powered when measuring resistance.
  • You can damage a multimeter by measuring voltage while it's set to resistance.
  • Connect an ohmmeter across a large, discharged capacitor and you'll see the resistance increase over time, as the meter's small current charges the capacitor up.

Soldering

  • Use rosin-core solder for electronics. Never use acid-core solder, which is meant for plumbing and corrodes circuits.
  • A cold solder joint (one that didn't get hot enough) looks rough or lumpy. A good tin-lead joint is smooth and shiny.

Key facts

  • Voltmeter: voltage, in parallel. Ammeter: current, in series.
  • Multimeter: voltage and resistance.
  • Ohmmeter: applies a small current and measures the voltage. Circuit must be unpowered.
  • Measuring voltage on the resistance setting can damage the meter.
  • Discharged capacitor on an ohmmeter: resistance increases with time.
  • No acid-core solder. Cold joint = rough or lumpy.
