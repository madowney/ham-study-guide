Radio waves don't just travel in a straight line and arrive cleanly. They bounce, get absorbed, and combine with copies of themselves. This lesson covers what happens to signals on the way, mostly at VHF and UHF.

Multipath: signals taking several routes

A signal often reaches you by several paths at once: directly, and bounced off buildings, hills or cars. Those copies arrive slightly out of step and cancel or reinforce each other. That's multipath, and it explains several things:

  • Moving your VHF antenna just a few feet can change the signal strength a lot.
  • Picket fencing: the rapid flutter on a signal from a moving car, as it passes through strong and weak spots.
  • Data transmissions: error rates are likely to increase.
  • Signals bounced off the ionosphere also fade irregularly, from the random combining of signals arriving by different paths.

Reflections can help, too. If a building blocks your path to a repeater, aim a directional antenna to find a path that reflects the signal to it.

Absorption

  • Vegetation absorbs UHF and microwave signals, so trees and leaves hurt weak-signal reception.
  • At microwave frequencies, precipitation (rain, snow) can reduce range.
  • On the lower 10- and 6-meter bands, fog and rain have little effect.

Polarization in practice

Recall that polarization is the direction of the electric field.

  • Long-distance CW and SSB on VHF/UHF use horizontal polarization. FM and repeaters use vertical.
  • If one station is vertical and the other horizontal (cross-polarized), over a line-of-sight path the received signal is much weaker.
  • Signals that come down from the ionosphere are jumbled (elliptically polarized), so either a vertical or a horizontal antenna works for HF skywave contacts.

The ionosphere

High above the weather, the sun electrically charges a layer of the upper atmosphere called the ionosphere. It can reflect (bend back) HF radio waves to Earth, letting them travel around the world. You'll see more of this in the next lesson.

Key facts

  • Multipath = copies cancel or reinforce: strength changes over a few feet, picket fencing, more data errors.
  • Irregular ionospheric fading = signals combining from different paths.
  • Blocked repeater path: find a reflected path.
  • Vegetation absorbs UHF and microwaves. Precipitation cuts microwave range. Fog and rain barely affect 10 and 6 m.
  • Weak-signal VHF/UHF CW and SSB: horizontal. Cross-polarized = weaker. Skywave: either polarization works.
  • The ionosphere reflects HF.
