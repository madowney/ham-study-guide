To carry a voice or data, a radio changes its signal in some way. That change is called modulation. Different kinds of modulation take up different amounts of spectrum (bandwidth), and that difference is what most of these questions test.

The main voice modes

  • AM (amplitude modulation): the signal's strength goes up and down with your voice. It's the oldest voice mode.
  • SSB (single sideband) is a form of AM. It strips out the parts of an AM signal that carry no information and keeps just one "sideband." That makes it efficient and narrow.
  • FM (frequency modulation), or its close cousin PM (phase modulation): your voice wiggles the frequency. It's clear and simple to tune, but wider.

Which mode where

  • VHF/UHF repeaters: FM or PM.
  • VHF packet radio (data): also FM or PM.
  • Long-distance, weak-signal voice on VHF/UHF: SSB.
  • SSB on 10 meters, VHF and UHF: upper sideband (USB) by convention.

Bandwidth: how much room each signal takes

CW (Morse code) is the narrowest of all, about 150 Hz. That's why it gets through when nothing else will.

SSB versus FM

  • SSB's advantage: narrower bandwidth.
  • FM's disadvantage: only one signal can be received at a time. The strongest signal "captures" the receiver, while on SSB you can hear several overlapping stations.

Key facts

  • SSB is a form of AM. 10 m, VHF and UHF use upper sideband.
  • Repeaters and VHF packet: FM/PM. Weak-signal VHF/UHF voice: SSB.
  • Bandwidths: CW ~150 Hz · SSB ~3 kHz · FM 10–15 kHz · fast-scan TV ~6 MHz.
  • CW is the narrowest. SSB is narrower than FM.
  • FM's drawback: only one signal at a time.
