Public service and emergencies are a big part of why amateur radio exists. This lesson covers the emergency organizations, how nets run, and how formal messages are passed.

The rules still apply

The FCC's Part 97 rules always apply, even when you're working with RACES, ARES or FEMA. One special exception: in situations involving the immediate safety of human life or protection of property, you may operate outside your license's frequency privileges if that's what it takes.

How a net works

A net is an on-air meeting. The Net Control Station runs it: it calls the net to order and directs the communications between stations that check in.

  • Standard practice: unless you're reporting an emergency, transmit only when net control directs you to.
  • Traffic means formal messages passed between stations.
  • For unusual words, names or places, spell them using the standard phonetic alphabet.

Formal messages (radiograms)

A formal message has a header, called the preamble, that holds the information needed to track the message: its number, priority, origin station and so on. One field in it is the check, which is the number of words (or word equivalents) in the message text. The receiving station counts the words to confirm nothing was lost.

Email over radio

Winlink sends email over ham radio, using addresses based on call signs (like KF1ABC@winlink.org). It's widely used in emergencies when the internet is down.

Key facts

  • FCC rules always apply. Outside your privileges only for the immediate safety of life or property.
  • RACES = civil defense, under Part 97; it requires civil defense certification.
  • ARES = licensed hams volunteering for public service.
  • Net control calls the net to order and directs traffic. Transmit only when directed, except for an emergency.
  • Traffic = formal messages. Preamble = tracking info. Check = word count.
  • Spell unusual words phonetically. Winlink = email by call sign.
