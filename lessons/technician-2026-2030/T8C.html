Hams do more than chat. This lesson covers hunting hidden transmitters, contesting, grid squares, and the systems that link radio to the internet.

Finding transmitters

Radio direction finding locates the source of interference or jamming. It's also a sport: a hidden transmitter hunt ("fox hunt"). The essential tool is a directional antenna, which picks up signals best from one direction, so you turn it until the signal peaks.

Contesting

Contesting means contacting as many stations as possible in a set time. Good practice: send only the minimum needed, which is your call sign and the contest exchange. Don't shorten your call sign, and don't make duplicate contacts.

Grid squares

A grid locator is a letter-number code for a geographic location, like "FN31." Hams exchange them to show roughly where they are, especially on VHF.

Radio meets internet

  • VoIP (Voice over Internet Protocol) means delivering voice over the internet digitally.
  • IRLP (Internet Radio Linking Project) connects ham systems such as repeaters over the internet. You access an IRLP node over the air with DTMF tones from your keypad.
  • EchoLink lets you transmit through a repeater without using a radio, from a computer or phone app. Before using it you must register your call sign and provide proof of license.
  • A gateway is a ham station that connects other ham stations to the internet.

Key facts

  • Direction finding locates interference. Fox hunt: a directional antenna.
  • Contesting = as many contacts as possible in a set time; send only the minimum.
  • Grid locator = a letter-number code for a location.
  • VoIP = voice over the internet. IRLP links repeaters over the internet, accessed with DTMF.
  • EchoLink: no radio needed; register your call and prove your license.
  • Gateway = connects ham stations to the internet.
