Amateur radio is for two-way, non-commercial, open communication. Most of the "what can't I send?" rules follow from that one idea, plus a few specific exceptions the exam asks about.

Who you can talk to

You can contact hams almost anywhere in the world. The exception: a country whose government has told the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) that it objects to such contacts. It's the ITU that matters, not the ARRL or IARU.

No broadcasting

The FCC defines broadcasting as transmissions intended for reception by the general public. Hams may not broadcast. Some one-way transmissions are allowed, though: Morse code practice, announcements of ham events, and telemetry or telecommand.

Ham stations can help news organizations only in one case: when the information is directly related to the immediate safety of human life or protection of property, and no other way to send it exists.

No secret codes

Messages encoded to hide their meaning are prohibited, with one exception: control commands sent to space stations or radio-controlled model craft. Ordinary digital modes like FT8 are fine, because their formats are public and not meant to hide anything.

Content rules

  • Obscene or indecent language: prohibited. There's no official word list; it's simply not allowed.
  • Music: not allowed, except when it's incidental to an authorized retransmission of manned spacecraft communications (for example, relaying an astronaut's audio that has music in the background).
  • Selling equipment: you may announce that you're selling or trading amateur radio equipment, as long as you're not doing it on a regular basis, which would be running a business.

No pay, with an exception

Hams generally can't be paid for operating. The exception the exam asks about: a teacher may operate as part of classroom instruction at an educational institution.

Two definitions

  • Auxiliary station: a station sending one-way transmissions between parts of a system, for example from a repeater's remote receiver site to its main transmitter.
  • Identification: you must identify your station when making test transmissions. The only time you may transmit without identifying is when controlling model craft (a radio-controlled plane, boat or car).

Key facts

  • Banned countries: those that notified the ITU of objection.
  • Broadcasting = meant for the general public. Prohibited. Code practice and event announcements are OK.
  • Encoded messages only for space station or model craft control.
  • Obscene language: prohibited. Music: only incidental to manned-spacecraft retransmission.
  • Selling ham gear: OK if not on a regular basis.
  • Paid operation: OK as part of classroom teaching.
  • News support only for immediate safety of life or property.
  • No ID needed only for model craft control. Test transmissions must be identified.
