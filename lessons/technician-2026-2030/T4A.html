This lesson covers putting a station together: powering a radio, connecting meters, hooking a computer up for digital modes, and wiring a radio into a car.

Powering a radio

Most mobile and base radios run on 13.8 volts DC, the voltage of a car's electrical system with the engine running. A transmitter draws a lot of current, so a 50-watt mobile FM radio needs a supply rated about 13.8 V at 12 amps. A 4-amp supply is too small, and 24 volts is the wrong voltage.

Use short, heavy-gauge (thick) power wires. Thin, long wires lose voltage when the radio draws high current on transmit, which is called voltage drop.

How long will a battery last? Divide the battery's amp-hour rating by the average current the equipment draws. A 20 amp-hour battery running a radio that averages 2 amps lasts about 10 hours.

In the car

Connect the radio's negative lead at the battery's chassis ground (where the battery's negative cable meets the car body), not to any random metal part, and not through the mounting bracket.

Meters

  • An RF power meter or SWR meter goes in the feed line, between the transmitter and the antenna.
  • When choosing an SWR meter, make sure it's rated for the frequency and power level you'll measure at.

Digital modes with a computer

Modes like FT8 run as software on a computer, using the radio's audio. The interface between computer and radio carries three signals: receive audio, transmit audio, and transmitter keying (the push-to-talk).

  • The radio's audio output goes to the computer's audio input: computer "line in" connects to the radio's speaker output.
  • The radio's audio input comes from the computer's audio output.

Think of it as a conversation: the radio's "mouth" (speaker) goes to the computer's "ear" (line in), and the other way around.

A hotspot is a small box that links nearby digital radios to a digital voice or data network over the internet.

Grounding and bonding

To tie equipment together electrically at radio frequencies, the preferred conductor is flat copper strap. Its wide surface works better at RF than round wire or braid.

Morse code helper

An electronic keyer helps you send Morse code by hand: hold a paddle one way and it sends perfectly timed dots, the other way and it sends dashes.

Key facts

  • A 50 W mobile radio needs 13.8 V at 12 A. Use short, heavy wires to minimize voltage drop.
  • Battery time = amp-hours ÷ average current.
  • Mobile negative lead goes to the battery chassis ground.
  • Power and SWR meters go in the feed line. Choose one by frequency and power.
  • Digital interface: receive audio, transmit audio and keying. Computer line in ← radio speaker.
  • RF bonding: flat copper strap. Hotspot: connects to a digital network. Keyer: helps send CW.
