Amateur radio exists for a purpose, and it has a referee. This lesson covers why the service exists, who runs it, how your license shows up, and a handful of definitions the exam likes to ask about.

Why amateur radio exists

The FCC rules for hams are called Part 97. Section 97.1 lists the "Basis and Purpose" of the service: public service and emergency communications, advancing the radio art, advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art, building a pool of trained operators and technicians, and international goodwill.

It is not a personal communications service for everyone (that's more like CB or FRS radio), and it is not for contesting as an end in itself.

Who's in charge

The FCC (Federal Communications Commission) writes and enforces the rules in the U.S. The ARRL is a national hams' association; it's helpful, but it isn't a regulator.

The rules are broad on purpose, and one of the few things they flatly forbid in every situation is willful or malicious interference: deliberately jamming or disrupting other stations.

Getting your license

After you pass, the volunteer examiners send your paperwork to the FCC. The FCC then sends you an email with a link to download your license grant; nothing comes by mail. What actually proves you're licensed is that your license appears in the FCC's ULS database (Universal Licensing System). A printed certificate from the exam session doesn't prove it.

Phonetics

When you give your call sign by voice, letters like B, D, E, P, T and V sound alike. The FCC encourages (but doesn't require) the standard phonetic alphabet: Alfa, Bravo, Charlie, Delta… So "KF1ABC" becomes "Kilo Foxtrot One Alfa Bravo Charlie."

Definitions the exam asks about

  • Space station: an amateur station more than 50 km above Earth's surface. The International Space Station's ham radio counts; so do ham satellites.
  • Beacon: a station that transmits automatically so others can check propagation. On HF, automatic beacons belong on 10 meters, between 28.200 and 28.300 MHz.
  • Frequency coordinator: a volunteer who recommends which channels repeaters should use, so repeaters don't step on each other. The coordinator is recognized by local amateurs and chosen by the hams in that area whose stations could be repeaters, not appointed by the FCC.
  • RACES (Radio Amateur Civil Emergency Service): emergency communications for government civil defense. To be a RACES control operator you need your license plus certification that you're enrolled in a civil defense organization.

Key facts

  • Purpose includes advancing skills in the technical and communication phases of the radio art.
  • The FCC regulates and enforces. The ARRL does not.
  • License notification: an FCC email with a download link. Proof: listed in the ULS database.
  • Phonetics are encouraged for phone ID, not required.
  • Space station: above 50 km. HF beacons: 28.200–28.300 MHz.
  • Frequency coordinators are chosen by local amateurs who could run repeaters.
  • RACES control operators also need civil defense enrollment.
  • Willful or malicious interference is always prohibited.
