More power means more chances for your signal to leak into places it shouldn't: your neighbor's stereo, your own microphone, the metal case of your radio. This lesson covers how to spot interference and how grounding and bonding fix it.

What RF interference sounds like

When your transmitter gets into an audio device, it sounds like this:

Your modeWhat the neighbor hears
SSB voiceDistorted speech
CWOn-and-off humming or clicking
  • A bypass capacitor can reduce RF getting into audio circuits by shunting RF to ground.
  • A ferrite choke on the cable reduces interference from common-mode current on an audio cable.
  • Interference across a wide range of frequencies may come from arcing at a poor electrical connection.

Ground loops and hum

If people tell you there's hum on your transmitted signal, a ground loop in your audio connections may be the cause. To minimize ground loops, bond all equipment enclosures together.

RF in the shack

  • Bonding all enclosures together also minimizes RF "hot spots."
  • RF burns can happen when the ground wire has high impedance at that frequency. A long ground wire can act like an antenna.
  • If the ground connection is resonant on your operating frequency, you can get high RF voltages on the equipment cases.

Safety grounding

  • All metal enclosures must be grounded so hazardous voltages can't appear on the chassis.
  • Don't solder lightning ground connections: the heat of a lightning strike would likely destroy the joint. Use clamps or welding instead.

Key facts

  • Interference from SSB: distorted speech. From CW: on-and-off humming or clicking.
  • Bypass capacitor and ferrite choke reduce RF in audio. Wideband interference: arcing.
  • Hum on your signal: ground loop. Fix it by bonding enclosures together, which also reduces hot spots.
  • RF burns: high-impedance ground wire. Resonant ground: RF voltage on the cases.
  • Ground metal enclosures so the chassis can't become live. No solder on lightning grounds.
