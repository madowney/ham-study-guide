How much power you can use depends on the band. The rule is simple once you see it: 1500 watts almost everywhere, with two special cases on 30 and 60 meters.

How power is measured

FCC power limits are stated as PEP output from the transmitter. PEP is peak envelope power, the strongest point of your signal. It's measured at the transmitter's output, not at the antenna and not as an RMS average.

The limits

WhereLimit
Most bands, including 160 m (1.8 MHz), 12 m and 10 m (28 MHz)1500 W PEP
30 meters (10.100–10.150 MHz, so 10.140 MHz too)200 W PEP
60 meters100 W effective radiated power, compared with a half-wave dipole

Default answer: 1500 watts. Switch to 200 watts only if the frequency is on 30 meters (it starts with 10.1).

60 meters: special rules

On 60 meters amateurs share fixed channels with government users, so the rules are tighter:

  • Upper sideband voice (USB) signals may be no wider than 2.8 kHz.
  • The power limit is effective radiated power, so antenna gain counts. If you use an antenna other than a dipole, you must keep a record of its gain.

New digital modes

You can invent and use a new digital protocol on the air, without asking the FCC first. The only requirement: publicly document its technical characteristics, so others can decode it.

Key facts

  • Power limits are PEP output from the transmitter.
  • 1500 W PEP on most bands: 160 m, 12 m, 10 m and so on.
  • 30 m (e.g. 10.140 MHz): 200 W PEP.
  • 60 m USB: 2.8 kHz maximum bandwidth. Using a non-dipole antenna: record its gain.
  • New digital protocol: publicly document it.
