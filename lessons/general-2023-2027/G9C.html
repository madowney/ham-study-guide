A Yagi is the classic rooftop beam: a row of elements on a boom that focuses your signal in one direction. This lesson covers how it works and the vocabulary for describing it.

Anatomy of a Yagi

  • The driven element, the one connected to the feed line, is about ½ wavelength.
  • The reflector is longer and the director is shorter. The beam points toward the director.
  • A longer boom with more directors gives more gain.
  • Larger-diameter elements give more bandwidth.
  • You can tune forward gain, front-to-back ratio and SWR bandwidth by adjusting all of these: the boom length, the number of elements and the element spacing.

Describing the pattern

  • Main lobe: the direction of maximum radiated field strength.
  • Front-to-back ratio: the power in the main lobe compared to the opposite direction.
  • Stacking two Yagis, spaced ½ wavelength apart vertically, gives about 3 dB more gain. Doubling the antennas doubles the power in the main lobe.

dBi and dBd

Gain is compared with a reference. dBi compares with an isotropic antenna, a theoretical point radiating equally in every direction. dBd compares with a dipole. A dipole already has 2.15 dB of gain over isotropic, so the dBi figure is 2.15 dB higher for the same antenna.

Matching a Yagi

  • Beta or hairpin match: a shorted stub of transmission line at the feed point.
  • Gamma match: the driven element doesn't need to be insulated from the boom. It can be bolted straight to the metal.

Key facts

  • Driven element about ½ wavelength. Reflector longer, director shorter.
  • Longer boom and more directors: more gain. Fatter elements: more bandwidth.
  • Boom length, element count and spacing all tune performance.
  • Main lobe: direction of maximum radiation. Front-to-back: main lobe vs. the opposite direction.
  • Two stacked Yagis: about 3 dB more gain. dBi = dBd + 2.15.
  • Hairpin: a shorted stub. Gamma: no insulation needed from the boom.
