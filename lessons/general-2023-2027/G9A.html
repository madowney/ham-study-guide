The feed line carries power from your radio to the antenna. This lesson explains impedance matching, SWR and loss, and how to calculate SWR in your head.

Characteristic impedance

Every feed line has a characteristic impedance, set by its physical shape. For parallel-conductor line (two wires side by side), it depends on the distance between the conductors' centers and the radius of the conductors.

  • Coax: usually 50 Ω.
  • Window line, a ladder-like twin lead: about 450 Ω.

Reflections and SWR

Reflected power comes from a mismatch between the feed line impedance and the antenna's feed point impedance. To prevent standing waves, the antenna's feed point impedance must match the feed line's characteristic impedance.

SWR for a resistive load: divide the larger impedance by the smaller one.

LineLoadWorkSWR
50 Ω200 Ω200 ÷ 504:1
50 Ω10 Ω50 ÷ 105:1

SWR is always written as a number to 1, never "1:4" or "1:5". Those answer choices are traps.

Antenna tuners don't fix the feed line

Say the feed line's SWR is 5:1 and you adjust a tuner at the transmitter until the radio sees 1:1. The SWR on the feed line itself is still 5:1. The tuner only makes the transmitter happy; the mismatch at the antenna end is unchanged.

Loss

  • Feed line loss is given in decibels per 100 feet.
  • Coax loss increases with frequency.
  • High SWR increases loss in a lossy line, because the power travels the line several times.
  • Loss also hides SWR: higher loss reduces the SWR measured at the input. Reflected power is weakened on its way back. A very lossy line can look like a perfect match!

Key facts

  • Parallel-line impedance: conductor spacing and radius. Window line: 450 Ω.
  • Reflected power: feed line and feed point impedances don't match. Prevent standing waves by matching them.
  • SWR = bigger ÷ smaller. 50 Ω into 200 Ω = 4:1. 50 Ω into 10 Ω = 5:1.
  • Tuner at the radio: the feed line SWR stays 5:1.
  • Loss: dB per 100 feet. It rises with frequency and with high SWR.
  • More line loss = lower SWR reading at the input.
