On any given path at any given moment, there's a window of frequencies that will get through. This lesson explains that window, MUF on top and LUF on the bottom, and how far one hop reaches.

MUF and LUF

  • MUF: Maximum Usable Frequency between two points. Above it, signals pass through the ionosphere and are lost to space.
  • LUF: Lowest Usable Frequency between two specific points. Below it, signals are absorbed before they reach the destination.
  • Between the LUF and the MUF, signals are refracted back to Earth. That's the usable window.
  • The least attenuation for long-distance skip is just below the MUF.
  • The MUF depends on all of these: path distance and location, time of day and season, and solar radiation and ionospheric disturbances.
  • If the LUF rises above the MUF, the window closes: ordinary skywave propagation on that path isn't possible.

Checking the bands

  • The best way to see current propagation from your station is to use an internet network of automated receiving stations, such as the Reverse Beacon Network or PSKReporter. They show where your signal is actually being heard.
  • If a signal arrives by both short path and long path, you may hear a slightly delayed echo.
  • In summer, the lower HF bands have high levels of atmospheric noise, or static, from thunderstorms.

Key facts

  • MUF = maximum usable frequency between two points. LUF = lowest usable frequency between two specific points.
  • Between LUF and MUF: refracted back. Below LUF: absorbed. Best: just below the MUF.
  • MUF depends on path, time, season and the Sun (all of these).
  • LUF above MUF: no ordinary skywave on that path.
  • One hop: E region about 1,200 miles, F2 about 2,500 miles.
  • Check propagation with automated receiving networks on the internet.
  • Short path plus long path: slight echo. Summer low bands: static.
