The two most common HF antennas are the dipole and the vertical. This lesson covers their patterns, their impedance, and how long to cut them.

Cutting to length

Two formulas give lengths in feet, with f in MHz:

½-wave dipole ≈ 468 ÷ f · ¼-wave vertical ≈ 234 ÷ f

½-wave dipole, 14.250 MHz468 ÷ 14.2533 feet

The dipole

  • In free space, its pattern is a figure-eight at right angles to the wire. Little goes off the ends.
  • When the antenna is less than ½ wavelength high, its pattern at high elevation angles is almost omnidirectional. Low dipoles radiate mostly upward.
  • As you lower a dipole toward 1/10 wavelength above ground, its feed point impedance steadily decreases.
  • Moving the feed point from the center toward an end makes the impedance steadily increase.
  • Horizontal antennas have lower ground losses than verticals.

The vertical

  • A quarter-wave ground-plane vertical is omnidirectional in azimuth: equal in every compass direction.
  • On an elevated ground plane, you get a 50 Ω feed point by sloping the radials downward.
  • On a ground-mounted vertical, the radials go on the surface, or buried a few inches deep.

Random wire

A random-length wire connected straight to the transmitter is easy to put up, but your station equipment may carry significant RF current. You could get RF burns or a "hot" microphone.

Key facts

  • Dipole = 468 ÷ f feet: 33 ft at 14.25 MHz, 132 ft at 3.55 MHz. Quarter-wave = 234 ÷ f: 8 ft at 28.5 MHz.
  • Dipole in free space: figure-eight broadside. Below ½ wavelength high: nearly omnidirectional at high angles.
  • Lower dipole: impedance decreases. Feed point toward the ends: impedance increases.
  • Horizontal polarization: lower ground losses.
  • Ground-plane vertical: omnidirectional. Slope the radials down for 50 Ω. Ground-mounted radials go on or just under the surface.
  • Random wire fed directly: RF current on your equipment.
